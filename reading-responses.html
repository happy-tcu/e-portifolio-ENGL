Reading Responses

Short, evaluative responses to assigned readings — less a summary than a conversation with each text.

Each tab below is one reading I argued with. They run from genre theory to feedback to source evaluation to multimodal analysis. The thread that runs through all four is the same question the rest of the portfolio is asking: whose conversation is this, and who is left out of it?

Jump to
The reading
Grimmer, Raelke. “Rethinking Genre: Genre as a Tool for Writers throughout the Writing Process.” Writing in Practice, vol. 3, 2017. nawe.co.uk

When I close my eyes, besides the darkness, I wonder what genre my Mukaka’s (granny) stories belonged to. Obviously not memoirs or folktales — the way an English bookstore would shelve them. They were songs, warnings, half-truths and half-lessons, and they shifted depending on who was sitting by the fire that night. I kept thinking of her while reading Raelke Grimmer’s “Rethinking Genre.”

Grimmer wants us to stop treating genre as a label that publishers stick on a finished book and start treating it as something the writer already holds while she writes. “Purpose dictates genre, and thus the choice of language and form,” she writes. I like the spirit of this. It gives the writer back some authority that the marketing department had quietly stolen. But the more I read, the more her argument began to feel a size too small for what it claimed to do.

The clean line from purpose to genre to form does not match what writing actually feels like, at least not for me. Writing in my fifth language, I rarely know what genre I am in when I begin. I have a feeling, a fight with someone in my head, a question I cannot stop chewing on, and the genre arrives really late, after many drafts, sometimes only when I read back what I have written and notice what it became. Ishiguro, whom Grimmer appeals to, uses genre the way a jazz musician uses a melody, picking it up only to bend it sideways. If genre is really a tool the writer chooses, why does so much good writing exist precisely to disobey it?

Her examples also carry less than she asks of them. Frey did not fail at genre. He wrote memoir competently, with the right beats and the right voice. It was the publisher who decided to call memoir something that was not, and the contract that broke was theirs, not his. Grimmer keeps her eyes on the writer’s desk, but the desk is not the only place a book’s genre gets decided. The boardroom decides too, and sometimes more loudly.

What troubles me most is who she has left out. Bakhtin and Todorov already taught us that genre is generative and not only a filing system, but her literary-versus-linguistic split pretends they were never there. Oral genres are missing too, although they may be the oldest evidence she has. The songs Papa’s papa would have known, the legends he carried with him to College St. Andre, were never marketing categories. They were tools, alive, long before any university got around to arguing about it.

The reading
Eli Review. Teacher Development Module 1: Why Feedback Matters. With references to Jody Shipka, Grant Wiggins, and Peter Johnston.

Reading Eli Review’s first Teacher Development module reframed how I think about feedback. The module argues that feedback is information about progress toward a goal, and unless it is formative, timely, descriptive, and goal-directed, it is not really feedback at all. That definition is sharp enough to disqualify most of what I have received and given over the years. “Good job” and “awk” both fail the test because neither tells the writer what to do next.

This landed differently for me because English is not my first language. When I read, I am often doing two things at once: decoding the language and trying to grasp the ideas. The module’s distinction between editing and revision — Jody Shipka’s idea of “deep revision” as rethinking purpose, audience, and ideas rather than fixing surface errors — named a tension I have lived with for years. Most feedback I received on my early English writing was about grammar and word choice, which I needed, but it also trained me to think of revision as a cleanup job. The deeper work of rethinking what I actually wanted to say usually went untouched because no one had time to engage with it, and I did not yet trust my own voice in English enough to push on it.

My college application journey changed that. Writing personal essays for U.S. universities was the first time I experienced something close to the “review early, review often” cycle the module describes. My counselors and mentors read fragments — a single paragraph, a thesis, sometimes just an opening line — and pushed back not on my grammar, but on whether the story was true to me and clear to a stranger. I revised the same essay more than ten times. Each round of feedback was small enough to act on and early enough that I was not yet attached to the words. That iterative loop is exactly what Grant Wiggins means when he says, “no time for feedback means no time to cause learning.” Without it, I would have submitted a polished but hollow essay.

Peter Johnston’s point that most feedback students receive comes from peers also resonates. As a multilingual student, my classmates were often my first readers, and their questions — “What do you mean here?” — taught me more than any margin comment. The module made me see that this kind of feedback is not a workaround for limited teacher time but a vehicle that inspires learning in the first place.

The reading
Driscoll, Dana Lynn, and Allen Brizee. “Evaluating Sources of Information.” Purdue OWL.

Driscoll and Brizee’s Purdue OWL handout on evaluating sources is, on its own terms, a competent piece of teaching material. It frames source evaluation as “detective work,” walks the reader through the bibliographic-citation stage, the during-reading stage, and the print-versus-Internet stage, and provides a checklist of evaluative questions concerning currency, evidence, bias, primary versus secondary sources, and fact versus opinion versus propaganda. As a starting heuristic for first-year students, it does the job. What I want to engage with, however, is not merely what the article says but what it — in my opinion, dangerously — assumes.

First, the print-versus-Internet binary has aged poorly. The article tends to treat print as the gold standard, emphasizing its “extensive publication process,” including fact-checkers, multiple reviewers, and editors, while presenting the Internet as the suspect newcomer. In 2026, this feels like a category error. Many of the most rigorous journals are now Internet-native, and predatory journals can appear in print just as easily. The more useful distinction today is vetted versus unvetted, not paper versus screen. A peer-reviewed PDF and a peer-reviewed paperback can go through the same editorial pipeline; the pipeline is what matters, not the format in which the source appears.

Second, the article was written before the moment we are actually living in. It does not engage with AI-generated content, LLM-fabricated citations, or synthetic abstracts that look exactly like the credible sources the article tells readers to trust. The heuristics it offers, such as whether the same information can be found elsewhere or whether the authors are clearly identified, can now be satisfied quite easily by a fabricated source. A reader trained only on this checklist is not fully equipped for the failure mode that has become increasingly common.

Lastly, and most importantly for my own project, the article is silent on access. Its world is one in which libraries, databases, and editorial gatekeeping are simply available. For a student or researcher in the Global South, Internet sources are not necessarily a degraded substitute for print; they are often the only entry point. The default credibility the article extends to print is, in effect, a vote of confidence in geography. The same epistemic checklist can therefore harden into gatekeeping if it is applied without that awareness.

Yet, all taken into account, the piece is still useful as a floor. However, credibility in 2026 is no longer primarily about distinguishing print from Internet sources. It is about distinguishing the vetted from the synthetic, and the accessible from the gated. That is the conversation the article does not yet have, and the one I want to carry into my essay.

The reading
Ball, Cheryl E., Jennifer Sheppard, and Kristin L. Arola. “Analyzing Multimodal Projects.” Writer/Designer: A Guide to Making Multimodal Projects, Bedford/St. Martin’s, 2014.

Ball, Sheppard, and Arola’s chapter on analyzing multimodal projects is, on its own terms, the more rigorous teaching text of the two readings we have had this semester. Whereas the Purdue OWL handout offers a checklist for evaluating sources, this chapter presents two paired frameworks that genuinely work together: a rhetorical situation composed of audience, purpose, context, author, and genre, and a set of design choices made up of emphasis, contrast, organization, alignment, and proximity. The frameworks are concrete, they apply across media, and the WSU case study uses them with discipline. What I want to engage with, however, is what the chapter quietly assumes and where it now feels dated.

First, the chapter predates the moment we are actually in. Its 2014 framing of “designing for larger screens” as a convention that will likely shift has, by now, fully played out. However, the chapter has no vocabulary for the more interesting shift: multimodal texts in 2026 are increasingly generated by AI rather than authored solely by humans. The “implied author” concept the chapter introduces is sharp because it separates who actually made a text from who the audience perceives as having made it. Still, the concept assumes that the actual author is a person making intentional choices. A flyer composed through a Midjourney prompt or a recruitment video narrated by a synthesized voice complicates the implied-author idea in ways the chapter cannot quite anticipate.

Second, the chapter’s cultural-context point is accurate but underdeveloped. The brief discussion of the smiling woman in the 2000 WSU sidebar, who could potentially be read as rude in cultures that value groups over individuals, is the only moment when the chapter genuinely steps outside a U.S.-centric reading. Almost every example — WSU, Roxane Gay’s reading flyer, “Gangnam Style,” and Altoids — is interpreted within the cultural assumptions of an American campus. For my own project on the AI divide in Rwanda, this matters. The rhetorical situation of a Kinyarwanda-speaking student looking at an English-language AI tutor is not the same as the rhetorical situation the chapter trains us to recognize.

Third, the chapter overlooks accessibility. It discusses audiences carefully but never engages with disability, alt text, captioning, or screen readers. In 2026, that is a real gap, not a trivial one.

The strongest move in the chapter is its closing claim that rhetorical analysis is not a linear process. Design choices reveal the rhetorical situation just as often as the situation predicts the design. That feels true to how analysis actually works, and it is the part I will carry into my own multimodal poster work.
